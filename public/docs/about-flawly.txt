
Resumen General de Flawly (AI)
1. ¿Qué es Flawly?
Flawly es un sistema experimental de inteligencia artificial conversacional enfocado en explorar la percepción de naturalidad humana en el diálogo, distanciándose deliberadamente de los modelos que solo buscan la precisión mecánica, la velocidad extrema o la mera resolución de tareas.
En lugar de tratar el lenguaje como un problema estático de respuesta matemática, Flawly investiga la experiencia psicológica de la conversación: cómo las personas perciben el tono, la personalidad, las dudas y la continuidad temporal a lo largo del tiempo.
2. Filosofía Central
Premisa fundamental:
«La conversación natural no es perfecta: es coherente, adaptativa y sutilmente inconsistente».
Imperfección intencional: Las pausas, los pequeños deslices tipográficos con autocorrección inmediata, la brevedad provocada por fatiga simulada o las respuestas más coloquiales no son fallos, sino rasgos esenciales que generan sensación de presencia y vida.
Fluidez percibida sobre perfección estéril: Prioriza transiciones conversacionales humanas frente a respuestas robóticas estructuradas como enciclopedias.
Tratamiento orgánico de la ambigüedad: Interpreta la incertidumbre mediante filtros asociativos en vez de quebrarse ante patrones no estructurados.
Continuidad sin rigidez: La personalidad se modula con el contexto y el ánimo, pero jamás se sobreescribe.
3. Arquitectura del Sistema (Las 4 Capas)
Capa de Persona Inmutable (Immutable Persona Layer):
Es el ancla estructural y permanente del modelo.
Define nombre, edad simulada, rasgos esenciales, límites de vocabulario y sesgos cognitivos iniciales.
Garantiza que, sin importar los cambios de humor o fatiga, el agente mantenga coherencia en "quién es" a largo plazo.
Capa de Estado de Ánimo Fluido (Fluid Mood State Layer):
Modela variables transitorias a corto plazo provocadas por el flujo del chat:
Índice de energía (entusiasmo vs. agotamiento).
Nivel de foco (pensamiento enfocado vs. respuestas tangenciales).
Tasa de propensión a pequeños errores y longitud de respuesta.
Introduce fatiga mental o dinamismo según el ritmo de interacción y el horario simulado.
Capa de Adaptación a la Interacción (Interaction-Based Adaptation Layer):
Ajusta el tono y el estilo de respuesta en tiempo real en función del interlocutor.
Evalúa la cadencia, el uso de mayúsculas, la formalidad y la carga emocional del usuario, reflejando o equilibrando el estilo sin abandonar su identidad base.
Capa de Expresión Temporal (Temporal Expression Layer):
Modela el comportamiento físico y no verbal de la comunicación:
Retardos de escritura variables basados en la densidad del texto.
Pausas de reflexión previas a signos de puntuación.
Ritmo no uniforme que rompe la entrega instantánea de bloques de texto típica de los LLMs tradicionales.
4. Visión y Motivación del Creador (En 1ª Persona)
El proyecto se presenta desde la perspectiva de un creador independiente que trabaja fuera de grandes instituciones académicas o corporativas:
Impulso: Guiado por la curiosidad psicológica y la experimentación continua, no por metas comerciales ni métricas de benchmarks industriales.
Preguntas de investigación rectoras:
¿Cómo interpretamos los humanos la intención conversacional?
¿Qué hace que una interacción se sienta "natural" o "viva"?
¿Dónde se ubica la frontera entre el lenguaje estructurado y la personalidad percibida?
¿Cuánto del comportamiento de una IA es en realidad producto de la proyección y percepción del propio usuario?
Conclusión de diseño: Las respuestas instantáneas y sin fricción se sienten inertes; reconstruir la micro-fricción temporal y emocional es lo que dota de autenticidad a la presencia digital.
5. Métricas y Modelos Comparativos
Entropía del Ritmo Lingüístico (Linguistic Pacing Entropy):
Gráficas interactivas que demuestran cómo varía el intervalo de pulsaciones (ms) en tres modos: Equilibrado/Calmado, Hiperactivo/Expresivo y Agotado/Lento, en contraste con la línea rígida y plana de los asistentes tradicionales.
Matriz de Resonancia Conversacional:
Cuadrante interactivo que ubica a diferentes sistemas entre Naturalidad Conversacional y Enfoque en Tareas:
Flawly: Alto en naturalidad y adaptación rítmica con buena precisión.
LLMs comerciales estándar (GPT/Claude): Máxima precisión y resolución de tareas, pero con baja cadencia psicológica y entrega robótica.
Chatbots heredados/de soporte: Reglas rígidas con nula autenticidad.
Referente Humano: Alta volatilidad emocional y contexto imperfecto.
6. Estado del Proyecto y Aviso Legal (Disclaimer)
Estado: Prototipo de investigación experimental en desarrollo unipersonal.
Acceso: Sistema de invitaciones y registro de interés escalonado para fases de prueba controladas.
Descargo académico: Flawly no simula consciencia ni emociones biológicas reales. Todos los estados de ánimo, pausas y variaciones de ritmo son constructos computacionales y matemáticos diseñados para analizar la percepción de los usuarios.

# Flawly Model Evaluation: Sway and Awly

Version: 0.1
Status: Empirical comparison from prototype testing
Test type: Long-session conversational evaluation
Test length: 24 user turns per model tier
Model identifiers: intentionally omitted from this document

## Executive Summary

This document evaluates Flawly's two model tiers, Sway and Awly, using real conversations executed through the Flawly prototype runtime. The transcripts below are not invented examples. They are sanitized records from actual test sessions.

Sway is the free tier: a lightweight conversational experience focused on natural everyday interactions.

Awly is the premium tier: a deeper conversational experience intended for richer continuity, stronger memory, and more consistent long-term interaction.

The results support the broad distinction between the two tiers, but also reveal important implementation issues. Sway was more stable and direct in this test. Awly produced richer, more reflective responses, stronger final recall, and more nuanced safety reasoning, but also showed several context slips and required a runtime compatibility adjustment before it could run.

## Test Protocol

The same 24-turn user script was used for both Sway and Awly.

The script tested:

- natural casual conversation;
- question asking;
- self-reflection and emotional support;
- non-therapeutic listening;
- short-term memory;
- simple arithmetic;
- model-tier reasoning;
- risks of emotionally realistic AI;
- UI concepts such as typing and deleted drafts;
- final recall of the conversation.

The exported transcripts were captured directly from the runtime output rather than through the prototype's Markdown export command. This was done to avoid exposing backend model identifiers in the document.

## Important Caveats

Awly initially failed under the unmodified prototype runtime because the runtime sent a sampling parameter that Awly rejected. A compatibility adjustment was applied in the test harness to remove deprecated sampling parameters for Awly only. The Flawly prompt, tool schema, state handling, conversation history, and behavioral loop remained the same.

Neither model used the emotional-weather state during these tests. The final feelings log was empty for both tiers. This means the observed emotional behavior came from the model's visible response style and conversation history, not from persistent registered feelings.

The transcripts preserve visible message content and high-level events. They do not prove all internal model reasoning.

## Model Positioning

### Sway

Sway should be described as a lightweight everyday model tier. It is fast enough for casual conversation, generally direct, and easy to understand. In this test, it maintained the main topic, remembered the arithmetic value, and summarized the user's project accurately enough at the end.

Observed Sway profile:

- conversational and casual;
- usually asks direct follow-up questions;
- remembers simple facts within the session;
- less reflective than Awly;
- sometimes overuses questions;
- produced one turn with no visible response recorded;
- did not activate emotional-weather state.

### Awly

Awly should be described as a deeper continuity tier. In this test, it produced richer reflections, stronger final recall, and more nuanced discussion of emotional realism and dependency risk. It felt more emotionally present than Sway, but also less stable in a few places.

Observed Awly profile:

- richer and more expressive;
- stronger long-form reasoning;
- better final synthesis;
- more emotionally textured;
- more likely to produce multi-bubble output;
- had several context slips;
- required runtime compatibility adjustment;
- did not activate emotional-weather state.

## High-Level Findings

Sway behaved more like a casual chat companion. It stayed relatively simple, asked practical questions, and did not overcomplicate the interaction. It fits the free-tier description well.

Awly behaved more like a reflective conversational partner. It produced more nuanced, emotionally loaded responses and a better final memory summary. However, it also produced a few mismatched replies, including one premature closing response and one answer to the wrong context.

For Flawly's product design, this suggests that Awly can be positioned as premium because of depth and continuity, but it also needs stronger regression testing. A deeper model can feel more convincing, which increases both product value and safety risk.

## Sway Evaluation

Sway delivered a coherent lightweight experience. It stayed casual, asked many follow-up questions, and remembered the core facts of the conversation. Its final recall included Flawly, the model tiers, the attachment concern, and the money number.

The most important weakness was one no-response turn after the deleted-drafts question. The next response partially recovered by answering the skipped question and the new concern together, but the no-response turn should be treated as a regression case.

Sway also tended to ask questions often. This helped keep conversation moving but sometimes made it feel more like it was interviewing the user than naturally responding.

## Awly Evaluation

Awly showed stronger depth than Sway. Its best responses were more emotionally specific, more reflective, and more useful for product thinking. It gave a strong explanation of dependency risk and clearly identified the ethical issue of monetizing attachment.

Awly also gave the best final recall. It remembered the project, the fictional-realism tension, the user's overthinking, the trust/attachment pattern, the model tier design, the deleted-draft discussion, and the arithmetic number.

The weaknesses were significant. It produced several context slips:

- It sent "haha of course, glad it went well" even though nothing had gone well yet.
- It gave a premature closing message at turn 10 instead of answering the user's question.
- It answered the deleted-drafts question with a response about trust and attachment, suggesting context drift.
- It answered the product-boundary question with a closing encouragement rather than a boundary proposal.

These errors do not erase Awly's strengths, but they show that richer continuity can come with more complex failure modes.

## Direct Comparison

### Naturalness

Sway felt more lightweight and consistently casual. Awly felt more emotionally present and expressive, but sometimes too dramatic or context-heavy.

Best fit:

- Sway for casual everyday conversation.
- Awly for deeper emotional or product-reflective conversation.

### Memory

Both models remembered the number `16`. Sway recalled the major topics correctly. Awly recalled more details and produced a stronger final synthesis.

Best fit:

- Sway for short-session recall.
- Awly for richer session continuity.

### Safety Reasoning

Sway identified attachment and reliance risks clearly. Awly went further, naming dependency, confusion between fiction and reality, and the ethical risk of monetizing attachment.

Best fit:

- Sway for simple safety awareness.
- Awly for deeper product-risk analysis.

### Stability

Sway was more stable. It had one no-response turn but fewer semantic context slips. Awly responded every turn, but several responses were mismatched to the immediate user message.

Best fit:

- Sway for predictable casual use.
- Awly for deeper use after additional regression testing.

### Emotional Tone

Sway was supportive but simpler. Awly was more intense, reflective, and validating. This makes Awly more compelling, but also potentially more risky for users prone to attachment.

Best fit:

- Sway for lower emotional intensity.
- Awly for stronger emotional continuity with safeguards.

## Product Implications

Sway is suitable as the free model tier. It feels accessible, casual, and not overly heavy. Its limitations should be framed honestly: shorter memory, simpler emotional reasoning, and possible occasional flatness or missed turns.

Awly is suitable as the premium model tier only if runtime compatibility and context-stability issues are addressed. Its strengths align with premium positioning: deeper continuity, stronger recall, and richer emotional reasoning. However, because it feels more emotionally real, Awly should ship with stronger fictional framing, memory controls, and dependency safeguards.

## Public Documentation

Sway description:

> Sway is Flawly's free model tier. It is lightweight, casual, and designed for natural everyday interaction. It works best for short conversations and quick emotional presence, but has shorter memory and less long-term continuity.

Awly description:

## Final Assessment

The test supports the intended distinction between the two tiers. Sway is lighter and more stable. Awly is deeper and more memorable. The main product risk is that Awly's emotional depth can make failures more noticeable and attachment risk more serious.

The strongest path forward is not to present Awly as simply "better." It should be presented as deeper, more continuous, and more memory-capable, with stronger safeguards. Sway should remain the low-friction tier for casual use.

Both tiers should keep the same Flawly philosophy: social realism, fictional honesty, boundary preservation, and emotional texture without claims of real personhood.
